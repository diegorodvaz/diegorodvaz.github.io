Why the gradient is perpendicular to level curves

Diego E. Rodríguez V. · 5 min read

If you look at the level-curve map in the gradient descent simulator, the ∇f arrows always cross the curves at a right angle. It isn't a coincidence or a quirk of the drawing: it's a property you can prove in two lines.

The proof

Let $f(x, y)$ be a differentiable function and let $f(x, y) = c$ be one of its level curves. We parametrize that curve as $\mathbf r(t) = (x(t),\, y(t))$. Since the whole curve lies on level $c$, for every $t$:

$$f\big(\mathbf r(t)\big) = c.$$

We differentiate both sides with respect to $t$. The right side is a constant, so it gives zero. On the left we apply the chain rule:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\, f\big(\mathbf r(t)\big) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,x'(t) + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\,y'(t) = \nabla f\big(\mathbf r(t)\big)\cdot \mathbf r'(t) = 0.$$

The vector $\mathbf r'(t)$ is tangent to the level curve. A zero dot product between two nonzero vectors means they are perpendicular. Therefore:

$$\nabla f \perp \text{the level curve at every point.}$$

Assumptions used

$f$ differentiable, the curve smoothly parametrized with $\mathbf r'(t) \neq \mathbf 0$, and $\nabla f \neq \mathbf 0$ at the point. Where $\nabla f = \mathbf 0$ (a critical point) there is no direction to compare: that's exactly where gradient descent stops.

The intuition: the directional derivative

The directional derivative measures how much $f$ changes if you move in the direction of a unit vector $\mathbf u$:

$$D_{\mathbf u} f = \nabla f \cdot \mathbf u = \lVert \nabla f \rVert \cos\theta,$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between $\nabla f$ and $\mathbf u$. Three facts follow from this formula at once:

  • Walking along the level curve doesn't change $f$, so $D_{\mathbf u} f = 0$. Since $\lVert\nabla f\rVert \neq 0$, we must have $\cos\theta = 0$: the tangent makes a 90° angle with the gradient.
  • The steepest ascent happens when $\cos\theta = 1$, that is, in the direction of $\nabla f$, and its rate is $\lVert \nabla f \rVert$.
  • The steepest descent happens when $\cos\theta = -1$: the direction of $-\nabla f$. That's why gradient descent takes steps $\mathbf x_{k+1} = \mathbf x_k - \alpha\,\nabla f(\mathbf x_k)$.

A practical consequence: the zigzag

The fact that $-\nabla f$ is the direction of steepest local descent doesn't mean it points at the minimum. The two only coincide when the level curves are circles.

Circular paraboloid: a straight path

With $f = x^2 + y^2$ the level curves are circles and $\nabla f = (2x,\, 2y)$ points radially. So $-\nabla f$ points straight at the origin and the algorithm descends in a straight line.

Elliptic paraboloid: a zigzag

With $f = \tfrac12(x^2 + 3y^2)$ the curves are ellipses and $\nabla f = (x,\, 3y)$. The $y$ component weighs three times as much, so $-\nabla f$ no longer points at the minimum (that's what the figure shows). One step of the algorithm becomes:

$$x_{k+1} = (1 - \alpha)\,x_k, \qquad y_{k+1} = (1 - 3\alpha)\,y_k.$$

With $\alpha = 0.5$ we get $x_{k+1} = 0.5\,x_k$ but $y_{k+1} = -0.5\,y_k$: the $y$ coordinate flips sign at every step. The point bounces from one side of the valley to the other as it moves toward the minimum. Also, convergence requires $|1 - \alpha| < 1$ and $|1 - 3\alpha| < 1$, that is, $0 < \alpha < \tfrac23$. With a larger $\alpha$, the steepest direction (the $y$ one) diverges.

Try it in the simulator

Pick Elliptic paraboloid (zigzag) and press Start: you'll see the bounce. Then raise α above 0.67 and watch it diverge. With Circular paraboloid the path is straight.

A drawing detail

The perpendicularity only shows if both axes use the same scale. If the $y$ axis is stretched relative to the $x$ axis, right angles get distorted. That's why the simulator's level-curve map locks a 1:1 scale between the axes.

In three variables

The same argument works for $F(x, y, z)$: any curve lying on the level surface $F = c$ satisfies $\nabla F \cdot \mathbf r'(t) = 0$. So $\nabla F$ is perpendicular to every tangent direction of the surface, which is why it's the normal vector of the tangent plane:

$$\nabla F(P_0)\cdot (\mathbf x - P_0) = 0.$$
