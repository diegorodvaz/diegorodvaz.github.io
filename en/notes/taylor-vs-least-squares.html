Taylor versus least squares

Diego E. Rodríguez V. · 5 min read

There are two ways to approximate $\sin x$ with a polynomial. Taylor uses the derivatives at a single point and looks at no data. Least squares looks at noisy points and finds the polynomial that fits them best. Both give a polynomial of the same degree, but they behave very differently. The code is in codigo/taylor-minimos-cuadrados, and there is an app to play with the parameters (its interface is in Spanish).

The experiment

We generate 20 points $(x_i,\ \sin x_i + \varepsilon_i)$ with $x_i$ drawn at random from $[-\pi, \pi]$ and Gaussian noise with standard deviation $0.25$. With scikit-learn we fit polynomials of degree 1 to 12 (PolynomialFeatures + LinearRegression). The errors go into a long-format pandas DataFrame that seaborn plots directly:

df = errores(x, y, max_grado=12)     # columns: grado, conjunto, rmse
sns.lineplot(data=df, x="grado", y="rmse", hue="conjunto", style="conjunto", markers=True)

There are three series. The training error is measured on the 20 noisy points. The test error is measured on the exact curve $\sin x$ at 400 points of $[-\pi, \pi]$ that the model never saw. The third is the error of the degree-matched Taylor polynomial at 0, measured the same way.

What the errors show

  • Least squares overfits. The training error only goes down: from $0.127$ at degree 3 to $0.106$ at degree 12. The test error does the opposite: it stays at $0.147$ between degrees 3 and 6 and climbs to $0.433$ at degree 12. The high-degree polynomial clings to the noise.
  • With little data and noise, a low degree wins. Least squares is fine at degree 3, while degree 3 Taylor has an error of $0.640$. Fitting across the whole interval pays off more than being exact at one point.
  • But Taylor ends up winning. It goes from $0.018$ at degree 7 to $0.0015$ at degree 9 and to $0.00009$ at degree 11. No fit to noisy data gets below $0.146$, because the noise sets a floor.

The underlying difference

Taylor is local and exact: it needs to know the function and its derivatives at a point, has no noise and no overfitting, and its error grows like $|x|^{n+1}$ as you move away. Least squares is global and statistical: it doesn't need to know the function, only data, but it inherits the noise and overfits when the degree is high for the number of points. If you have the formula, use Taylor. If you only have measurements, fit and control the degree with held-out data.

What you see depends on the seed, the number of points and the noise. In the app you can change them and watch the best degree move.
